# Assignment 1. What is something you believe to be true that most people around you would disagree with?

### Rules

* No "safe" answers.
* Must be your real belief (not copied from internet).
* Minimum 50 words.

**Hint:** What do you believe about career, money, learning, discipline, relationships, health, success, life, tech industry, etc. that most people don't agree with?

## Answer
I believe that being good at something is not enough to succeed in a career. Most people around me believe that if we study well, get good marks, and build technical skills, opportunities will naturally come. I disagree with that. I believe we also need to learn how to communicate, explain our work, handle rejection, and keep improving after failure. I have realized that knowing something and being able to show others that you know it are two different skills. Sometimes, rejection teaches us more clearly what we need to improve than success does. For me, career growth is not about being perfect from the beginning; it is about continuously becoming better and not giving up when things don't work the first time.

**Truth:** (1 sentence)

**Evidence from my life:** (2–4 lines: what you tried + what happened)

---

## Truth #1

### Truth
Consistent practice improves performance more reliably than simply studying more.

### Evidence from my life

I practiced aptitude, SQL, Java, DSA, and coding questions repeatedly for placement tests.
When I only studied concepts, I struggled to solve questions under time pressure.
After practicing similar problems and learning from my mistakes, I became faster and more confident in coding and aptitude rounds.

## Truth #2

### Truth

Technical knowledge alone does not guarantee success in a selection process.

### Evidence from my life
I cleared some aptitude and coding rounds but was rejected in later technical, GD, or task rounds.
These experiences showed me that knowing programming concepts is only one part of getting selected.
Communication, explaining projects, problem-solving, and demonstrating practical knowledge also affect the final result.
---

## Truth #3

### Truth
Failure gives useful information when I analyze what caused it instead of treating it as the end.

### Evidence from my life

After different placement rejections, I looked at exactly where I lost marks or struggled instead of simply blaming the company or the difficulty.
I noticed gaps in areas such as DSA, project explanation, core concepts, and communication.
I then changed my preparation based on those gaps, which gave me a clearer direction for future interviews.

## Your Article

The Journey of K. T. Ahitha: From Placement Preparation to Building Real-World Technology
A few years ago, K. T. Ahitha was a computer science student who was preparing for placements while still figuring out what kind of developer she wanted to become. Today, she has built a strong career around software development, cloud technologies, DevOps, and practical problem-solving.

Her transformation did not happen because of one major breakthrough. It happened through years of consistently improving her technical skills and learning from real experiences. During her early career, she strengthened her foundation in Java, DSA, SQL, OOPS, Spring Boot, REST APIs, Git, and cloud technologies. Instead of only collecting certificates, she focused on building projects that demonstrated what she could actually do.

One of the projects she developed was CareerForge, a student career guidance and placement preparation platform. She built the backend using Java and Spring Boot, connected it with MySQL, designed REST APIs, and managed the project through GitHub. The project became an important part of her portfolio because it demonstrated her ability to take an idea from planning to implementation.

She also contributed significantly to a Smart Waste Segregation Compliance Monitoring System. The project used QR-based household identification, compliance tracking, violation records, photographic evidence, and a dashboard to address a practical waste-management problem. She learned how to work within a team, divide responsibilities, solve implementation problems, and explain technical decisions clearly.

Her GitHub profile gradually became more than a collection of college assignments. It became evidence of her learning journey, containing projects, documentation, code, and experiments. She also published technical articles and project-related work, allowing others to understand not only what she built but also what she learned while building it.

DevOps and cloud learning became another important part of her development. Through the DevOps Micro Internship, she gained practical exposure to deployment, automation, cloud concepts, and modern development workflows. She completed relevant certifications and used those skills in projects rather than treating certification as the final goal.

Professionally, Ahitha moved into a software development role where she worked on real applications and collaborated with developers, testers, and other teams. Over time, she took ownership of features instead of waiting for someone else to define every step. She reviewed code, documented solutions, helped newer students and developers understand technical concepts, and eventually led small project teams.

Outside her job, she continued contributing to the developer community. She shared learning resources, wrote about her experiences, and helped other students understand placement preparation, Java, DSA, and project development. Her own struggles with interviews and rejections became useful lessons that she passed on to others.

The biggest change in her 2.0 version was not a job title or salary. It was the way she approached learning. She had moved from asking, “What should I learn next?” to asking, “What can I build, prove, and contribute with what I already know?”

Her portfolio, GitHub projects, certifications, published work, professional experience, and leadership responsibilities became proof of that transformation. What started as a student's effort to become placement-ready had developed into a career built around continuous learning, practical technology, and meaningful contribution.

# Assignment 4. Have you ever cut corners (unethical / dishonest / shortcut behavior — not necessarily illegal)? If yes, how did it make you feel?

### Important

You don't need to write the full story.

Focus on the feeling:

* guilt
* fear
* shame
* stress
* regret
* numbness
* etc.

This is about self-awareness, not judgment.

### Answer Format

**Yes**

If Yes:

**What emotion did you feel?** (minimum 50–100 words)

## Answer

The main emotions I felt were guilt and stress. When I took a shortcut instead of doing something properly, I initially felt relieved because I had saved time or avoided difficulty. But that feeling did not last long. I kept thinking about whether I had actually earned the result or just found an easier way to reach it. I also felt uncomfortable when I knew that I had not given my genuine effort. It taught me that shortcuts may solve an immediate problem, but they can create stress and reduce confidence in myself later. Since then, I have been trying to be more honest about what I know and what I still need to learn.

# Reflection – Week 1

### Biggest insight I got about myself this week

I realized that I have the motivation to improve, but I sometimes focus more on planning what I should do than consistently doing it. I also realized that every small improvement in skills like Java, DSA, SQL, communication, and project work adds up over time.

### My biggest weakness/loop I noticed

My biggest loop is trying to work on too many things at the same time. When I have many pending goals, I can feel overwhelmed and sometimes delay starting. I need to focus on one important task at a time instead of constantly thinking about everything that is still pending.

### One system I will implement from this week (exact habit + time)

Every weekday from 7:30 PM to 8:30 PM, I will have one distraction-free focus session. Before starting, I will choose exactly one task, such as solving DSA problems, studying Java/SQL, or working on a project. I will keep my phone away and record what I completed at the end of the session.
